## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
1. Did the Veterans' Judicial Review Act strip district courts of the jurisdiction, recognized by this Court in Johnson v. Robison, 415 U.S. 361 (1974), to hear challenges to the constitutionality of acts of Congress affecting veterans' benefits?

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 151 F.4th 1287. Held: the district court lacked jurisdiction over a veteran's constitutional challenge to 38 U.S.C. §5313 (reduction of benefits for incarcerated veterans), because the Veterans' Judicial Review Act channels such challenges through the VA, the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims and the Federal Circuit; Robison involved a materially different, since-amended statutory scheme.

## Procedural posture
Certiorari granted 6 April 2026 to the Eleventh Circuit. Petitioner, a veteran whose disability benefits were reduced during incarceration, sued in district court under 28 U.S.C. §1331 arguing §5313 is unconstitutional; the district court dismissed for lack of jurisdiction and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The joint appendix was dispensed with. Set for argument 5 October 2026.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Section 511(a) of the VJRA bars district-court review only of questions the VA must resolve to interpret and apply the benefits statutes; a decision "under" a statute is one applying it, not one on its validity, which is exactly how Robison read the identical predecessor language that Congress re-enacted.
- Structure confirms this: §511(a) also directs the Secretary to decide the covered questions, yet the Board of Veterans' Appeals cannot and does not decide the constitutionality of statutes (VA regulations forbid it), while Congress expressly gave the Federal Circuit that power; forcing standalone constitutional challenges through the years-long VJRA process would serve only delay, and any ambiguity is resolved by the pro-veteran canon.
- The Eleventh Circuit's reliance on Elgin v. Department of Treasury (2012) is misplaced: an implied-preclusion inquiry is unnecessary where Congress defined preclusion in express text, and even under Thunder Basin nothing in the VJRA withdraws jurisdiction over this claim.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Congress may displace §1331 jurisdiction and did so expressly: §511(a) directs the Secretary to decide "all questions of law … necessary to a decision" under a benefits law and bars review of "any such question" outside the VJRA's channels; whether §5313 is constitutional is necessary to the amount-of-benefits decision, and the Federal Circuit is expressly authorised to decide the validity of statutes within that scheme.
- Robison is distinguishable: the former §211(a) turned on whether a claim arose under a VA-administered law, whereas current §511(a) turns on whether a question is necessary to the Secretary's decision, and Robison predates the creation of the Veterans Court and Federal Circuit, so preclusion then meant no review at all, whereas now it means channelling.
- The Board's supposed inability to decide constitutional questions is overstated and irrelevant since higher levels of the scheme can; the VJRA is unambiguous so the pro-veteran canon does not apply; and the Thunder Basin factors (meaningful review, not wholly collateral, within agency expertise) point the same way.
